My story

In my own voice, from my portfolio. I’ll keep refining it.

A girl who loved hard questions

I was in Grade 8 in Kabul, competing in the Kangaroo Math Competition, when schools closed in 2021. I loved the subjects that hand you a problem and make you earn the answer: mathematics, physics, science.

Two years without a classroom

For almost two years I could not study in a classroom. I kept reading and teaching myself, until in 2023 I found an online school and started Grade 9. I finished Grades 9, 10 and 11 with A+ results, and was named Student of the Year, then recognized in Mathematics and Science.

Education has always been important to me, and I do not want the circumstances I face to stop me from learning.

Another door

After Grade 11 I learned my school could not issue the accredited diploma I need for university. The UWC Afghanistan National Committee had recommended the Amala Global Secondary Diploma to me in 2024, so I enrolled. It lets me finish secondary education online while I keep building.

Curiosity with no walls

I studied calculus, physics and aerospace engineering on my own, and I love aviation. I hope to fly one day. I turned what I learned into projects, and I turned my time into service: tutoring in maths and science, mentoring through Afghan Girls in STEM, founding Apex International MUN and teaching advanced English to Afghan girls.

What I learned

Losing a classroom taught me what one is worth. I don’t want to only receive an education. I want to use it, with people who see the world differently from me.

Why UWC

UWC’s mission is to make education a force to unite people, nations and cultures for peace and a sustainable future. I know what education is worth because I nearly lost it. I want to spend mine on that mission.

      Space and engineering

      LunarLens

      An interactive tool for understanding the engineering decisions behind future lunar missions.

      What it does

      Drag a 3D Moon, pick a landing site, and see the Sun’s and Earth’s elevation over the next 30 days. Sites are ranked by power (sunlight), Earth link (communication) and water ice, and you set the weights, so the ranking changes with your priorities.

      Why I built it

      Choosing where to land is an engineering tradeoff: no site wins on sunlight, Earth link and ice at once. I built it on the problem behind NASA’s Space Apps challenge about lunar landing sites. It is not affiliated with NASA.

      What it teaches

      Most places on the Moon get about two weeks of night. Libration lets Earth drift in and out of view at the edges. The far side never sees Earth. The poles trade long daylight for a grazing link.

      The model, honestly

      It uses the 29.53-day lunar cycle, the Moon’s axial tilt and approximate libration. It is a teaching model, not mission planning. Next I would use real ephemeris data, add terrain and illumination maps, and test the scoring against published analyses.

      Physics and learning

      STEMLab

      Interactive science experiments that make difficult concepts easier to understand.

      What it does

      Four live simulations: a projectile with adjustable air drag, an orbit you launch as a satellite, forces on a ramp with friction, and a “check your thinking” section. Graphs are interactive: drag or click to scrub through a flight or set the ramp angle.

      How it teaches

      Each lab has a “Try this” prompt. Why do 30° and 60° give the same range? With drag, is 45° still best? Raise a ramp until the block slips and check it against atan(0.4).

      The physics underneath

      The orbit lab switches between Leapfrog and Euler integration so you can watch an orbit spiral outward when the maths is poor, though the physics has not changed. Circular orbit speed is 1 and escape speed is about 1.41.

      Why it matters to me

      I tutor maths and science, and I wanted to build the tool I wish every student could play with.
